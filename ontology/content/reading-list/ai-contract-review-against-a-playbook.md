## What this list covers

A negotiation playbook is a legal team's written positions for each recurring clause, with the fallbacks it may give and who must approve anything beyond them. When a counterparty returns a draft, the reviewer checks every change against that playbook. In 2026 more of that first check is done by software, and the playbook has become the standard the software applies. This list covers what a playbook contains, how it is built and kept current, and why review is measured against it. It also covers the volume pressure behind automated review, what surveys say about AI in contract work, and the duty that stays with the lawyer. Two pieces are vendor marketing and two are vendor surveys, and each note says so.

## What a playbook is

Sterling Miller's "Ten Things" guide is the most practical account of building and maintaining a playbook. His tactics piece for Thomson Reuters places the playbook inside a wider discipline of preparation and knowing when to walk away. The ACC Jobline article gives a short definition: preferred positions, acceptable fallbacks and hard limits for each recurring clause. Spellbook's guide lists the same parts from a vendor's side and argues that a playbook should change with each negotiation.

## Applying it at volume

Legistify's article makes the vendor's case for consistency: the same approved position, applied by software wherever the same clause appears. CLOC's 2025 report explains why departments want that. Of the organisations it surveyed, 83% expected demand for legal work to increase.

## What the surveys say about AI in contracts

Conga's survey of 250 contract professionals found AI in near-universal use, with 92% still requiring a person to review the output. Zoho's survey of 1,151 professionals in ten countries found half still managing contracts by hand, and named negotiation a primary concern because of manual redlining.

## The lawyer's duty

The Bar Examiner's analysis of the American Bar Association's Formal Opinion 512 sets out the US lawyer's duty to check what an AI tool produces. The opinion is advisory. The analysis says the ABA's ethics committee issues opinions to guide courts, lawyers and the public.

## What to read first

Start with Sterling Miller's "Ten Things" guide, because it is free to read, written by a practitioner and explains how a playbook is built and kept current. Then read his tactics for Thomson Reuters, which show where the playbook sits in a negotiator's preparation. Then read the Bar Examiner's analysis of Formal Opinion 512, which explains why a lawyer still reviews whatever an AI agent finds against the playbook.
