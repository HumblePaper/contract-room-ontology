## Why the comparison comes first

A counterparty's tracked changes show only what the counterparty chose to mark. That is usually everything it changed, but not always. A full comparison against the version that was sent shows every difference between the two texts. So it is the only reliable basis for reviewing a returned draft. Running the comparison before reading the markup also gives the reviewer one document in which every change, marked or unmarked, can be reviewed in order.

## Running the comparison

Word's legal blackline, and the comparison functions in dedicated comparison software, take an original and a revised document. They produce a third document that shows every difference as a tracked change, and they leave both originals unchanged. Compare the returned draft with the exact version that was sent. A comparison against a later internal working draft will report the reviewer's own changes as if the counterparty had made them.

## Reviewing what the comparison shows

The reviewer reads every change in the comparison. Changes to definitions, cross-references, numbers and schedules deserve the closest reading, because a small edit to a definition can change how clauses elsewhere in the agreement work. Each change is accepted, rejected or countered. Every change that is not accepted goes into the issue list.

## Before signature

Run the same kind of comparison on the execution version against the last agreed draft before anyone signs. An unmarked change in a clean execution copy is the most costly form of this pitfall.
