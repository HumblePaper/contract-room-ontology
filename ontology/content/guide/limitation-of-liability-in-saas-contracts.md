## Why the cap decides SaaS negotiations

A SaaS vendor sells the same hosted service to many customers, for a fee that is small next to the harm a serious failure could do to any one of them. The limitation of liability clause settles that mismatch, so most SaaS negotiations turn on it. The vendor needs one cap it can offer every customer and insure. An enterprise customer needs a cap that bears some relation to its real loss if the vendor loses its data or the service fails.

## How a well-drafted clause is built

A well-drafted SaaS limitation of liability clause has four parts. The first part excludes named heads of loss (named kinds of loss), such as lost profits, lost revenue, loss of goodwill and indirect or consequential loss. It says whether each is excluded even when it is direct. The second part sets a general cap, usually tied to the fees paid and payable in the twelve months before the claim. The third part sets any super-cap (a higher cap for named claims), most often for breaches of the data protection and security obligations. The fourth part lists the carve-outs, meaning claims that neither the exclusions nor the caps reach. These are typically fraud, death or personal injury caused by negligence, and the intellectual property indemnity. The clause should say whether the cap applies per claim or in aggregate, and exactly how it applies to each indemnity.

## Where the negotiation usually lands

A vendor's standard position is a cap at twelve months' fees. Enterprise customers commonly get twenty-four months' fees or a multiple of the annual fees. Customers ask for uncapped liability for data protection breaches, and vendors usually offer a super-cap instead, sized against the vendor's cyber insurance. The intellectual property indemnity is commonly left uncapped. The vendor's liability for service availability is usually limited to service credits (fee discounts for downtime).

## The law behind the clause

In England and Wales, the Unfair Contract Terms Act 1977 requires a limitation of liability for negligence, or one in written standard terms, to be reasonable. Section 11(4) tells the court to consider the resources and insurance of the party relying on a cap. In India, section 73 of the Indian Contract Act 1872 excludes remote and indirect loss by statute, and Indian courts generally enforce caps agreed between commercial parties. In Delaware, courts enforce caps between sophisticated parties as written, subject to the rule that a party cannot exclude liability for its own fraud.

## Checking the clause in a returned draft

A vendor's counsel reviewing a customer's markup should first compare the returned draft with the draft that was sent. Changes to the cap, the carve-outs or the definition of Losses are among the changes most often made without tracking. The vendor's counsel should then read the limitation of liability clause, the indemnities and the definition of Losses together. All three should give the same answer for a data breach, an infringement claim and a service failure.
