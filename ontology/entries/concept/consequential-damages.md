## Definition

Consequential damages are losses that flow from a breach of contract only because of circumstances particular to the injured party. They do not arise naturally and directly from the breach. The classic statement comes from the English case of Hadley v Baxendale (1854), which divides recoverable losses into two kinds. The first kind arises naturally from the breach in the usual course of things. The second kind is loss that both parties, when they made the contract, would reasonably have expected as the probable result of the breach. Losses of the second kind are often called consequential or indirect losses. Lost profits on other contracts are the most common example.

## Why it matters at the table

Almost every commercial contract excludes indirect and consequential loss, and what that exclusion is worth depends on what the phrase means. That turns on the governing law and the contract's own definitions. A vendor wants the exclusion to reach lost profits, lost revenue, lost data and loss of goodwill, whether those losses are direct or indirect. A customer wants the exclusion to be narrow. Otherwise the losses that matter most to it, such as the cost of restoring data or replacing a failed service, may be swept in. A negotiation over the exclusion usually turns on the list of named kinds of loss, not on the general words.

## How clauses implement it

The exclusion usually sits in the limitation of liability clause. It is either a separate sentence before the cap or a paragraph listing the excluded heads of loss (named kinds of loss). A well-drafted exclusion names each head of loss it excludes. It says whether each is excluded when it is direct as well as when it is indirect, and it says which claims are carved out. Where an indemnity uses the defined term Losses, the definition often expressly includes or excludes consequential loss. That keeps the indemnity and the exclusion consistent.

## By jurisdiction

### England and Wales

English courts have traditionally read "consequential loss" in an exclusion as covering only losses of the second kind in Hadley v Baxendale. On that reading, direct lost profits stay recoverable. More recent decisions have been willing to read the phrase in its ordinary commercial sense, in the context of the whole contract. So careful drafters in England and Wales list the excluded heads of loss rather than relying on the phrase alone.

### India

Section 73 of the Indian Contract Act 1872 puts the rule in Hadley v Baxendale into statute. It says that no compensation is given for remote and indirect loss or damage. So an express exclusion of consequential loss in an Indian-law contract often restates the statutory position. Its practical effect depends on the specific heads of loss it names.

### Delaware

Delaware and other US courts generally treat consequential damages as damages that do not flow directly and immediately from the breach. They enforce contractual waivers of consequential damages between commercial parties. Whether lost profits are direct or consequential depends on the facts and the contract. So US drafters also name the excluded heads of loss expressly.
