## Standard definition

A typical definition of Losses reads: "Losses means all losses, liabilities, damages, costs, charges and expenses, including reasonable legal fees and the costs of investigation." In US practice the definition is often longer. It lists judgments, settlements, fines, penalties and interest, and it often excludes punitive or exemplary damages unless they are paid to a third party. The definition of Losses matters because an indemnity promises to cover Losses. So every word in the definition widens or narrows what the indemnifying party pays.

## Variants

A buyer-friendly or customer-friendly definition puts consequential loss, diminution in value (a fall in what an asset is worth) and lost profits inside Losses. An indemnity then reaches those losses even where the limitation of liability clause excludes them for ordinary claims. A seller-friendly or vendor-friendly definition expressly excludes indirect, consequential, special and punitive damages and lost profits. In acquisition agreements, the parties also negotiate whether Losses can be calculated on a multiple of earnings. That method can multiply the value of a claim many times over.

## Traps

The most common trap is a mismatch between the definition of Losses in the indemnity and the exclusion of consequential loss in the limitation of liability clause. Suppose the exclusion applies to the whole agreement and the definition of Losses includes consequential loss. Then the contract contradicts itself, and the outcome turns on which provision a court reads as the specific one. A second trap is a definition that includes "costs" without saying whether internal management time can be recovered. A third is a definition drafted for one document and copied into another where the indemnities serve a different purpose.

## Where it matters

The definition of Losses matters most in indemnities, in acquisition agreements where the seller indemnifies the buyer for breach of warranty, and in any contract whose cap or exclusion refers to Losses rather than to liability in general. A reviewer reads the definition together with the limitation of liability clause and the indemnity clause. The reviewer then confirms that the three provisions give the same answer for the claims that matter most to the client.
