## Overview

A priced equity round is a financing in which a company sells new shares to investors at a price per share agreed in a term sheet. The shares are usually a new series of preferred stock. The price per share and the number of shares outstanding before the round set the company's pre-money valuation (its value before the new money). A lead investor negotiates the terms, and other investors join on the same terms. Priced rounds are named by series, starting with Series A. Each round adds a new class of preferred shares with its own rights.

## Structure choices

The main structure choices in a priced equity round are the liquidation preference, the anti-dilution protection, the make-up of the board, the protective provisions that need investor consent, and whether the round closes at once or in tranches (stages). The liquidation preference is the investors' right to be paid an agreed amount first on a sale or winding up, usually one times the purchase price, non-participating. The anti-dilution protection is usually broad-based weighted average. At the same closing, SAFEs or convertible notes from earlier rounds may convert into the new series.

## Lifecycle

A priced equity round starts with the term sheet. Confirmatory due diligence and the drafting of the definitive documents follow, then signing and closing, usually on the same day. After closing, the company files its amended charter and issues share certificates or book entries. It also updates its capitalisation table and makes any required securities filings.

## Document set

The definitive documents in a US priced round usually come from the NVCA model documents. They are the stock purchase agreement, the amended and restated certificate of incorporation, the investors' rights agreement, the voting agreement, and the right of first refusal and co-sale agreement. Board and stockholder consents go with them, and a legal opinion or closing certificate where required.

## Parties and advisers

The parties to a priced equity round are the company, the lead investor and the other investors. The founders and existing major shareholders also sign, as parties to the voting and co-sale agreements. Which side drafts the definitive documents varies by market. In Silicon Valley practice, investor counsel commonly prepares the term sheet and company counsel drafts the definitive documents. In East Coast practice the convention varies, and counsel for the lead investor often drafts while company counsel marks up. The company's existing investors are usually represented through the lead investor's counsel or by their own counsel.

## Economics

The economics of a priced equity round come from five figures: the amount raised, the pre-money valuation, the size of the employee option pool, the liquidation preference and the anti-dilution protection. Whether the option pool is created before or after the round affects how much existing holders are diluted. The company usually pays the legal fees, including a capped amount of the lead investor's counsel fees.

## Where it goes wrong

A priced round goes wrong when the capitalisation table is wrong, when earlier SAFEs or notes convert on terms the parties have not modelled, when required consents from existing investors are missed, or when the protective provisions give investors a veto over ordinary business decisions. Diligence issues can also delay closing, such as founder intellectual property that was never assigned to the company, or a customer contract with uncapped liability.

## By jurisdiction

### Delaware

Most US venture-backed companies are Delaware corporations, and the NVCA model documents assume Delaware law. The rights of preferred stock are set out in the certificate of incorporation filed with the Delaware Secretary of State.

### India

An Indian priced round is documented in a share subscription agreement and a shareholders' agreement. The company's articles of association reflect the investors' rights. Investment by non-residents must follow the pricing and reporting rules under the Foreign Exchange Management Act 1999.

## By industry

### SaaS

Investors in SaaS companies look at annual recurring revenue, net revenue retention and gross margin. Their diligence reviews the company's standard customer terms, including the cap on liability and any customer contracts that depart from it.
